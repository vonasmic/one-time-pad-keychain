\large SE firmware (STM32U535 + TROPIC01), SAE node, and USER application}
\author{Michal Vonášek}
\date{31 October 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document is the security analysis of the QKD-backed one-time-pad keychain.
\begin{itemize}
\item The secure element (\emph{SE}) is an STM32U535 Cortex-M33 with TrustZone and a TROPIC01 secure chip, reached over USB CDC.
\item The provisioning node (\emph{SAE}) is a Java service with a Utimaco HSM. It talks to peer SAEs over mutually authenticated post-quantum TLS RMI.
\item The home-PC console (\emph{USER}) runs \code{ENCRYPT}, \code{DECRYPT}, and \code{MANAGE}.
\end{itemize}
The methods come first: scope, deployment assumptions, attacker models, and the scoring scheme (CVSS v3.1, CVSS v4.0, STRIDE). The architecture, the trust assumptions, and the threat model follow, then the controls, the residual findings, and the operational countermeasures. We check the architecture against what the STM32U535 hardware provides and every security property against its implementation.

The firmware core holds: the TrustZone split, the owner-pinned USER sessions, the OTP consume with its cursor re-read, verify-after-write, and advance-then-erase, and the PIN with MAC-and-Destroy and a constant-time tag check. The inter-SAE RMI layer authenticates callers by SPKI pin, authorizes mutations by the pair lease of the caller, binds one address, and filters deserialization of both call arguments and replies. Every finding is a residual risk that is either accepted by design or covered by an operational countermeasure. The highest-rated one is on the SE: the trust-anchor mutation (\code{CREDS SAE}) is protected only by the owner key, by product decision (\hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Executive summary}

\paragraph{Overall posture.} The SE keeps its main promise. Reading pad material out of the chip needs both the enrolled owner key (the TLS peer SPKI pin) and the PIN (TROPIC01 MAC-and-Destroy). A compromised NonSecure image, an arbitrary USB host, or a SAE does not have both. The residual risks are elsewhere:
\begin{enumerate}
\item On the SE, \textbf{\code{CREDS SAE}} lets anyone who holds the owner key replace the SAE CA that \code{PROVISION} trusts. That person can then act as a SAE towards this device and inject pads they know. It does \emph{not} give them \code{ENCRYPT}/\code{DECRYPT}, which still needs the PIN. This is accepted by design (\hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}).
\item A \textbf{forged ciphertext} that the user decrypts burns the decrypt pads up to a slot the attacker chooses, and a modified ciphertext decrypts to modified plaintext without detection. Ciphertexts therefore travel over an external authenticated channel (\hyperref[f:F-02]{\fid{F-02}}, \hyperref[m:M-1]{M-1}).
\item The \textbf{SAE--KME hop} uses CLASSICAL TLS while it carries pad plaintext. This matters under the post-quantum model~B, and the link runs on a private cable or segment (\hyperref[f:F-03]{\fid{F-03}}, \hyperref[m:M-2]{M-2}).
\item The \textbf{TROPIC01 L3 session} on the SPI bus is X25519 only. Under model~B, a recording of the bus during \code{PAIRING} yields the pairing key and full L3 access to TROPIC01. That access discloses no pads, PIN, or device secrets, but it allows denial of service and, together with the owner key and a recorded PIN entry, a reset of the PIN budget. The SE is a stock TROPIC01 USB devkit whose board cannot be changed, so \code{PAIRING} runs in operator custody before issue, the devkit is sealed, and it stays with its user (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}, \hyperref[m:M-3]{M-3}).
\item The \textbf{inter-SAE RMI service} faces a network that the deployment treats as untrusted and world-wide. Its controls hold (Section~\ref{sec:controls}):
  \begin{itemize}
  \item the caller is the SPKI-pinned TLS peer, never a call argument;
  \item every write or delete needs a pair lease held by the caller;
  \item the listener binds one configured address;
  \item call arguments, return values, and the registry stub pass allow-list serialization filters;
  \item the node does not start without a pin list.
  \end{itemize}
  The remaining RMI findings are Low or Info: unauthenticated handshake reachability, covered by a firewall allow-list (\hyperref[f:F-06]{\fid{F-06}}), and two accepted properties of the re-provision handover, the share deletion by a non-party (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}) and metadata readable by any pinned peer (\hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}).
\item \code{CertGenerator} and its CAs are lab-only tooling (\hyperref[f:F-13]{\fid{F-13}}); Section~\ref{sec:mit-ca} gives the production CA requirements.
\item Side-channel and fault exposure is analysed per primitive in \S\ref{sec:sca}. The PIN budget withstands MCU glitches because TROPIC01 enforces it, and the OTP cursor is re-read and verified after every write. The main physical residuals are unmasked ML-DSA-44 signing (device impersonation), for which wolfSSL has confirmed side-channel protection in a later release, and an RDP~2 bypass leading to page-22 readout.
\item The results rest on nine trust assumptions (\S\ref{sec:trust}): the hardware vendors, the third-party libraries, the HSM, the KME, the CAs, the SAE host and its administrator, an authentic peer-card exchange, the home PC and the user, and the toolchain and manufacturing. The peer card is the least obvious of them: it is an unsigned SHA-384 client hash that is checked only for format, so users compare it over an independent channel (\hyperref[ta:TA-7]{TA-7}).
\item The attacker capability matrix (\S\ref{sec:matrix}) shows that pad confidentiality depends on the SAE hosts and the KME link, while pad use on the device needs both the owner key and the PIN.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{CA verification result.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The SE is the TLS \emph{client} in every mode. It never verifies a client certificate against any CA.
\item In \code{PROVISION} it verifies the SAE's \emph{server} chain against the single CA stored on flash page~21. That is the CA written by \code{OWNER SET} or \code{CREDS SAE}.
\item In \code{ENCRYPT}, \code{DECRYPT}, and \code{MANAGE} it loads \emph{no} CA. The verify callback accepts any chain. The only identity check is a byte-exact, constant-time comparison of the peer leaf SPKI with the enrolled owner key (Section~\ref{sec:ca}).
\item The SAE Node already builds \emph{two separate} \code{SSLContext}s with disjoint truststores. The RMI context trusts only \code{root-ca}. The USB device-server context trusts only \code{client_ca}.
\item \code{CertGenerator} creates the two CAs as independent self-signed roots and refuses to give them the same name.
\item A device or client certificate therefore cannot authenticate to RMI. On top of the \code{root-ca} chain check, RMI pins each peer leaf by SPKI hash (\code{spkiSha384} in the SAE directory \code{SAE_NODES_FILE}), so a leaf under \code{root-ca} that is not pinned is rejected (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).
\end{itemize}
\section{Scope and deployment context}\label{sec:context}

\subsection{Target}
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[SE] \texttt{stm32u535-trustzone-usb/}: the Secure image (wolfSSL TLS 1.3, Tropic SPI, OTP logic, NSC veneers), the NonSecure image (USB CDC device, frame parser), linker scripts, \texttt{user\_settings.h}, and \texttt{SE\_firmware.ioc} for security attributes only.
\item[SAE] \texttt{JAVA\_APPS/modules/sae-node} (node, provisioning session, RMI, QKD client, SQLite, HSM TLS), \texttt{TerminalBridge} (USB framing), and \texttt{CertGenerator} where it creates CAs and node identities.
\item[USER] \texttt{JAVA\_APPS/modules/user-app}: files on disk, prompts, and network listeners.
\end{description}
Vendored trees (wolfSSL, libtropic, HAL/CMSIS, Utimaco jars) are trusted at their integration boundary (\hyperref[ta:TA-2]{TA-2}). The host-side \code{tropic_model} and \code{se_host} are lab stand-ins and are not targets. Real \code{env/*.env} files, PKCS\#12 files, PEM keys, and \code{pairing-key.hex} are deployment data, not part of the target. We analyse main repository commit \texttt{bbd5f32} (3 Nov 2026) with firmware repository commit \texttt{bc9c7ed} (4 Nov 2026); all line numbers refer to these commits.

\subsection{Deployment assumptions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The SAE runs on a dedicated server with a real Utimaco HSM. It does not use the simulator.
\item SAEs are distributed world-wide. The network between them is \textbf{not trusted}.
\item Production SEs are set to \textbf{RDP level~2} (\code{0xCC}) with per-device \code{OEM1KEY} and \code{OEM2KEY} regression keys provisioned first (\code{HOW_TO_RUN.md}, ``Lockdown and reverting''; referenced from \code{BUILD_AND_FLASH.md}). Hide protection (HDP) is not used. There is \textbf{no field firmware update path}. Only the holder of the OEM keys can regress the level and reflash, and replacing the Secure image erases the device secrets.
\item The SE is the stock TS13 TROPIC01 USB devkit running our firmware. Its board, and with it the MCU--TROPIC01 SPI traces, cannot be changed.
\item The user notices a failed \code{INIT} or enrollment. This lowers the likelihood of \hyperref[f:F-05]{\fid{F-05}}.
\item Lab artefacts are not present in production: \code{RDP=0xAA}, \code{BOOT_LOCK=0}, \code{INIT LAB} holding \code{client_ca.p12}, the simulator PIN, and the \code{127.0.0.1} peer table.
\item The PIN length is 8--16 characters: \code{SE_TROPIC_PIN_SIZE_MIN 8u} and \code{SE_TROPIC_PIN_SIZE_MAX 16u} in \code{se_tropic_pin.h}, enforced in \code{secure_otp.c}, \code{se_tropic_pin.c}, and \code{se_tropic_mlkem.c}.
\item Certificate revocation is a manual action at the CA.
\item \code{ENCRYPT}/\code{DECRYPT} happen only on the home PC, never at the SAE terminal.
\item \code{CREDS SAE} stays owner-only by product decision. The consequences are spelled out in \hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations of the evaluation}\label{sec:limits}
The evaluation is based on the source code, the build configuration, and the automated tests. It includes no power, EM, or timing measurements, no fault injection, and no fuzzing, so side-channel and fault statements describe exposure, not demonstrated leakage. wolfSSL, Bouncy Castle, libtropic, and the Utimaco provider are trusted at their integration boundary (\hyperref[ta:TA-2]{TA-2}), and silicon properties are trusted as documented by the vendors (\hyperref[ta:TA-1]{TA-1}). UserApp is covered for its files, prompts, and network listeners. The TROPIC01 bus statements (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}) are derived from the libtropic key schedule and the firmware call sequence, not from a bus capture. The firmware does not write the TROPIC01 R-config or I-config, so the per-slot user access privileges are the factory defaults, which we do not evaluate command by command. The CVSS vectors encode the preconditions as stated in each finding.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Attacker models}
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[Model A: classical attacker with access] This attacker can open USB CDC and act as any USB host, including BadUSB-style firmware. They can cut power, probe the board SPI, and keep a stolen SE indefinitely. They can be a network peer anywhere on the Internet path between SAEs. They can be a local user on the home PC, or someone holding its disk. They can obtain a certificate under one of the CAs if a SAE or a CA host is compromised.
\item[Model B: post-quantum attacker] Model A, plus the ability to break X25519 and P-256 (Tropic L3, ECDSA uplink signature, factory SH0, the CLASSICAL KME hop). This attacker cannot break ML-KEM-768, ML-DSA-44, AES-256-GCM, or SHA-384, and cannot bypass PIN plus MAC-and-Destroy by mathematics alone.
\end{description}
Every finding says which model it needs. An attack that works under model~A is not presented as a post-quantum result.

\subsection{Contexts}
Findings are qualified as \emph{locked SE} (production: \code{TZEN=1}, \code{RDP=0xCC} with OEM regression keys, no HDP, no field update), \emph{lab SE} (\code{RDP=0xAA}, \code{BOOT_LOCK=0}), \emph{production SAE} (dedicated host, real HSM, untrusted WAN between SAEs), \emph{lab SAE} (localhost, simulator), or \emph{home PC}.

\subsection{Scoring}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CVSS v3.1 and v4.0} base vectors are given for each finding. They are scored for the production context unless stated otherwise, and computed with the \code{cvss} Python package (FIRST equations). CVSS does not capture deployment controls such as the operator noticing a failed enrollment. The severity does.
\item \textbf{STRIDE} classifies the violated property: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege.
\item \textbf{Severity}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Critical}: disclosure of pads, PIN, device secrets, or CA/HSM keys, or a validation bypass that accepts a forged provision.
  \item \emph{High}: integrity failure of pad distribution, a later command that undoes an enrollment control, or SAE trust that follows a caller-supplied field.
  \item \emph{Medium}: inconsistent checks, an exposed attack surface, or oracles.
  \item \emph{Low}: self-limited corruption, availability visible to the operator, or hygiene.
  \item \emph{Info}: true and accepted, or without direct impact.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Treatment.} Every finding is a residual risk. It is either \emph{accepted} by design, with the reason stated, or covered by an \emph{operational countermeasure} that the operator or the user applies (Section~\ref{sec:countermeasures}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation approach}
We evaluate each layer against its security goal:
\begin{itemize}
\item the hardware capabilities of the STM32U535 against the CubeMX IOC and \code{user_settings.h} (Table~\ref{tab:hw});
\item the memory map and TrustZone attribution from the linker scripts, \code{partition_stm32u535xx.h} (SAU), and \code{MX_GTZC_S_Init} (TZSC, MPCBB) (Table~\ref{tab:map});
\item the NSC veneers, the USB frame parser, and the MANAGE and \code{OWNER SET} parsers for pointer and length handling;
\item enrollment against every later mutation of the owner SPKI, the reset-password hash, the SAE CA, the device certificate, the ML-DSA key, the pairing key, the peers, and the PIN and ML-KEM seed;
\item the TLS identity per mode on the SE, the OTP consume path, the PIN, secret lifetime, and the RNG;
\item SAE provisioning, the SAE truststores, the RMI surface, the QKD client, and the SAE host secrets;
\item the USER application's files and sockets.
\end{itemize}
The properties that hold are collected in Section~\ref{sec:controls} and Appendix~\ref{app:holds}; the residual risks are in Section~\ref{sec:findings}.

\subsection{Trust boundaries}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{TB\arabic*}]
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB1}\textbf{USB cable to SE NonSecure.} Any host. ASCII commands are unauthenticated. TLS modes are armed here.
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB2}\textbf{NonSecure to Secure (NSC).} The veneers are the only entry. Pointers are checked.
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB3}\textbf{Secure to TROPIC01 (SPI, L3).} X25519 Noise session over the devkit's on-board traces. The key is factory SH0 until \code{PAIRING}. Under model~B the session is open to anyone who recorded \code{PAIRING} (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}).
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB4}\textbf{SE to SAE (\code{PROVISION} TLS).} The SAE server chain is checked against the page-21 CA. The device mTLS leaf is checked against \code{client_ca}. The uplink is bound to the exporter.
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB5}\textbf{SE to USER (\code{ENCRYPT}/\code{DECRYPT}/\code{MANAGE} TLS).} The peer SPKI is pinned to the owner key. The PIN travels inside TLS.
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB6}\textbf{SAE to SAE (RMI over TLS, untrusted WAN).} Mutual TLS under \code{root-ca}.
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB7}\textbf{SAE to KME (QKD 014, CLASSICAL TLS).}
\item \phantomsection\label{tb:TB8}\textbf{SAE to HSM.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Certificate authorities and truststores (verification)}\label{sec:ca}

\paragraph{SE side (firmware).} In all four modes, \code{tls_start} in \code{se_tls_client.c} creates a \code{wolfTLSv1_3_client_method} context. The SE is always the TLS client.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\code{PROVISION}.} \code{tls_load_provision_ca} reads the CA DER from page~21 (\code{se_creds_get_sae_ca}) and loads it with \code{wolfSSL_CTX_load_verify_buffer}. Verification is \code{VERIFY_PEER | FAIL_IF_NO_PEER_CERT} with the default callback, so the SAE \emph{server} chain must validate to the stored CA. This is the same CA that \code{OWNER SET} and \code{CREDS SAE} write. The SE presents its device certificate and ML-DSA key as the client certificate.
\item \textbf{\code{ENCRYPT} and \code{DECRYPT}.} No CA is loaded. The verify callback \code{tls_accept_peer_leaf} returns~1 unconditionally. After the handshake, \code{se_tls_user_pin_peer} compares the peer leaf SPKI with the owner SPKI from NV, using a constant-time OR loop. The device certificate is still presented.
\item \textbf{\code{MANAGE}.} As for \code{ENCRYPT}: the same owner pin, no CA. The difference is that no device certificate is presented.
\end{itemize}
The SE never validates a client certificate against a CA, because it is never the TLS server. The CA set by \code{CREDS SAE} is used only to authenticate the SAE in \code{PROVISION}.

\paragraph{SAE side (Node).} \code{Node.java} (lines 144--145) builds two contexts from the HSM identity:
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{HsmNodeTls.createContextForNode} is the \textbf{RMI} context, installed into \code{RmiManager}. Its truststore contains only \code{root-ca.pem} (\code{TlsStores.rootCaPem}). It presents the chain \{node leaf, root-ca\}.
\item \code{HsmNodeTls.createContextForCommandServer} is the \textbf{USB device-server} context, used for \code{PROVISION}. Its truststore contains only \code{client_ca.pem} (\code{TlsStores.clientCaPem}). It presents the same node leaf chained to \code{root-ca}, which the SE then validates against its page-21 CA.
\item The QKD context (\code{createContextForQkd}) is a third, separate truststore holding the KME server CA.
\end{itemize}
\code{CertGenerator.provisionAll} creates \code{root-ca} and \code{client_ca} as two independent self-signed ML-DSA roots (\code{createCa}, \code{BasicConstraints(true)}). \code{CertGenConfig} rejects equal names. Node leaves are issued by \code{root-ca}. Device leaves are issued by \code{client_ca} (\code{SoftwareLeaf.signRawMlDsa44Leaf}).

\paragraph{Result.} The truststores are \textbf{separate, not merged}. A device or client certificate (\code{client_ca}) cannot authenticate to RMI, and a node certificate (\code{root-ca}) cannot act as a device in \code{PROVISION}. Three properties complete the picture:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item RMI pins its peers on top of the chain check. An inbound leaf must match an SPKI pin in the SAE directory (\code{SAE_NODES_FILE}), which yields the caller's SAE ID. An outbound server leaf must match the pin of the SAE configured for that endpoint (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}).
\item The same \code{root-ca} anchors both RMI and the SE's \code{PROVISION} check, so one CA key opens both doors. This is accepted; a production PKI can split the two roles (Section~\ref{sec:mit-ca}).
\item The relying parties do not check EKU, KeyUsage, or \code{CA:true}. Role separation rests on the issuer and, for RMI, on the pins. This is accepted, because the pins already bind every RMI peer to one key. Lab leaves (\code{CertGenerator}) carry no EKU or KeyUsage (\hyperref[f:F-13]{\fid{F-13}}); production certificates come from a production CA (Section~\ref{sec:mit-ca}).
\end{enumerate}
\section{Trust assumptions}\label{sec:trust}
The analysis relies on the following assumptions, which cannot be verified from the source. For each one it states what is trusted without verification, why the trust cannot be checked here, what follows if it is violated, and which matrix rows (\S\ref{sec:matrix}) and findings it relates to. They are in addition to the deployment assumptions in \S\ref{sec:context}.
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.2em]
\item[TA-1 Hardware vendors]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-1}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} On the STM32U535: RDP level~2 really disables SWD and readout and freezes the option bytes, leaving only the \code{OEM2KEY} regression request under reset; the OEM keys are write-only; TrustZone (SAU, IDAU, GTZC, MPCBB) separates Secure from NonSecure as documented; the TRNG produces full-entropy seeds; and the option-byte SRAM erase on reset and the TAMP erase work as documented. On TROPIC01: MAC-and-Destroy enforces the PIN budget against any party without an L3 session (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}); R-MEM slots are readable only through an authenticated L3 session; and the tamper and glitch countermeasures work as claimed.
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} These are silicon properties, outside a source-level evaluation (\S\ref{sec:limits}). The TROPIC01 countermeasures are vendor claims.
\item \textbf{If violated.} An RDP~2 bypass exposes page~22 in plaintext (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}) and turns ``SE only'' into ``SE + owner key'': the device key, pairing key, and owner pin become readable. A broken TrustZone lets a malicious NonSecure image act as Secure. An M\&D bypass removes the PIN as a second factor (rows ``SE + owner key'' and ``SE only''; \S\ref{sec:sca}). A weak TRNG weakens the device TLS and ML-DSA keys. Pad randomness does not depend on it (\hyperref[ta:TA-4]{TA-4}).
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-2 Third-party libraries]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-2}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The library versions taken from the build files:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Firmware: wolfSSL/wolfCrypt 5.9.4 (\code{Middlewares/Third_Party}), libtropic v4.1.0 (submodule tag), STM32Cube FW\_U5 V1.9.0 (IOC) with STM32U5 HAL driver 1.6.3, and the ST USBX CDC~ACM device stack from that package.
  \item SAE and UserApp (\code{JAVA_APPS/pom.xml}, Java release~21): Bouncy Castle 1.84 (\code{bcprov}, \code{bctls} for the \code{PURE_PQC} TLS, \code{bcpkix}, \code{bcutil}); the JDK's RMI and JEP~290 filtering; \code{sqlite-jdbc} 3.49.1.0 with HikariCP 7.0.2; Jackson 2.21.3, which parses the SAE directory \code{sae-nodes.json}; and jSerialComm 2.11.0 for UserApp USB.
  \item Build-time and test-only: Flyway 12.8.1 (\code{flyway-maven-plugin} schema migrations; \code{flyway-core} only in test scope).
  \item The Utimaco client: \code{cryptoservercxi.jar} and \code{securityserver-jce.jar}, vendored as system-scope jars with no version recorded in the build.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} These trees are trusted at their integration boundary (\S\ref{sec:limits}). The Utimaco jars are closed source.
\item \textbf{If violated.} A defect in wolfSSL or libtropic runs in the Secure world of an SE that cannot be updated. Every row whose ``Cannot'' column rests on TLS or L3 then collapses. A Bouncy Castle TLS defect breaks \hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1} and the \code{PROVISION} channel (rows ``Network MITM'' and ``Malicious peer SAE''). A JDK RMI or deserialization defect bypasses \hyperref[c:C-4]{C-4}. A defect in the Utimaco client can expose the wrap key (\hyperref[ta:TA-3]{TA-3}).
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-3 Utimaco HSM]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-3}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The wrap key that protects stored records is non-exportable. Only the SAE server administrator administers the HSM.
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} Key attributes and the HSM's user and role setup are outside the repository.
\item \textbf{If violated.} An exported wrap key, or a second administrator, turns row ``One SAE: root, no HSM access'' into ``One SAE: root, with HSM access'': the attacker can read all stored pads for the pairs that SAE serves.
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-4 KME / QKD]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-4}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The KME delivers key material that is secret and truly random, and it delivers the same keys to both SAEs of a pair. The SAE--KME link is CLASSICAL TLS (\hyperref[f:F-03]{\fid{F-03}}).
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} The QKD system and the KME are external. Only their ETSI~014 client is in scope. One-time-pad secrecy rests entirely on this material.
\item \textbf{If violated.} Biased, predictable, or disclosed material breaks pad confidentiality for every pair served by that KME (row ``QKD/KMS link''; \hyperref[f:F-03]{\fid{F-03}} under model~B). If the two SAEs receive different material, the pads of a pair do not match and decryption fails. This is an availability failure, not a confidentiality one.
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-5 Certificate authorities]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-5}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The SAE root CA (\code{root-ca}) and the client CA (\code{client_ca}) sign only legitimate SAEs and devices. Revocation is manual, and it happens soon after a loss.
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} The production CA is not in the repository. \code{CertGenerator} is lab-only (\hyperref[f:F-13]{\fid{F-13}}). Issuance and revocation are procedures, not code (\S\ref{sec:mit-ca}).
\item \textbf{If violated.} A rogue \code{root-ca} leaf is still refused over RMI unless its SPKI is also pinned (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}). However, any SE that trusts that CA through \code{PROVISION} accepts it as a SAE (rows ``SAE root CA compromised'' and ``SE + owner key''; \hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}, \hyperref[f:F-05]{\fid{F-05}}). A rogue \code{client_ca} leaf passes the SAE device check (row ``Client CA compromised''). Slow revocation extends each of these windows (\S\ref{sec:residual}).
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-6 SAE server and kiosk]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-6}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The SAE server and its terminal are physically protected. The administrator with root access is trusted, and so is the operating system.
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} Host hardening and staffing are outside the code. The SAE sees pad plaintext in RAM by design (\S\ref{sec:residual}).
\item \textbf{If violated.} Root on a SAE equals the pads: row ``One SAE: root, with HSM access'', or ``no HSM access'' when \hyperref[ta:TA-3]{TA-3} holds. A root attacker can also rewrite the SAE directory (\code{SAE_NODES_FILE}) and so choose that node's peers (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}).
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-7 Authentic peer-card exchange]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-7}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The SE never moves between peers. Two users exchange only a \emph{peer card}, and the assumption is that the card is not substituted in transit and that it is verified, for example by comparing the fingerprint over an independent channel.
\item \textbf{What the card is.} \code{PEER CARD} (UserApp \code{PeerCommands.card}, lines 77--90) prints a four-line paste block: \code{PEER ADD}, the nickname, the 96-hex \emph{client hash}, and the Tropic plaintext pad length. The client hash is read from the device with \code{CLIENT HASH}, and the pad length with \code{TROPIC PAD LEN} (lines 140--156). The client hash is SHA-384(SPKI of the device certificate $\|$ TROPIC01 ECC public key) (\code{se_tropic_session.c} 26--56). The card carries no certificate and no signature.
\item \textbf{How it is imported.} \code{PEER ADD} (\code{PeerCommands.add}, lines 105--138) checks only the format: a 1--16 character nickname, exactly 96 hex digits, and a pad length greater than zero. It then sends the entry over owner-authenticated MANAGE. The firmware (\code{se_manage.c} \code{do_peer_add}, 210--231) checks only the lengths and that the pad length is non-zero, then stores the entry. Each \code{PROVISION} uplink lists the stored peers (\code{se_tropic_session.c} 188--211). The SAE creates the record for (own client hash, selected peer hash), with pad length min(own maximum, peer pad length) (\code{InputHandler} 52--59). Neither the SE nor the SAE can tie a hash to a person.
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} The exchange happens out of band, between users. The only check available is a human comparison of the 96-hex value, for example against the other side's \code{CLIENT INFO}.
\item \textbf{If violated.} If an attacker who holds any enrolled device substitutes their own client hash, the victim's pads for ``Bob'' are shared with the attacker's device, and the attacker reads every message sent to ``Bob''. A substituted pad length only shortens messages or makes provisioning fail. The impact equals a man-in-the-middle at enrollment. No matrix row covers it, because the attacker needs neither the victim's SE nor the owner key.
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-8 Home PC and user]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-8}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The home PC is not compromised while \code{ENCRYPT} or \code{DECRYPT} runs. The user keeps the PIN and the owner-key PKCS\#12 password secret.
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} It depends on the user's platform and behaviour.
\item \textbf{If violated.} Rows ``Home PC compromised'', ``Stolen owner key only'', ``SE + PIN'', and ``SE + owner key + PIN'' apply. Malware can read plaintext and keylog the PIN while the SE is attached. A stolen owner key opens \code{CREDS SAE} (\hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}) and \code{PAIRING LOAD} (\hyperref[f:F-09]{\fid{F-09}}).
\end{itemize}
\item[TA-9 Toolchain and manufacturing]\phantomsection\label{ta:TA-9}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trusted.} The compiler, the linker, the build host, and the initial flashing are trusted. So is the factory step that provisions the OEM keys, sets RDP~2, enrols the device, and runs \code{PAIRING}. The OEM keys are random per device and are kept offline by the manufacturer, never in the repository. There is no field update, so the image flashed at manufacturing stays until the key holder regresses the unit.
\item \textbf{Why unverifiable.} No reproducible-build or attestation mechanism exists to compare a field unit against the source.
\item \textbf{If violated.} A modified image, or a unit left at RDP~0 or with the factory SH0 key (\hyperref[f:F-12]{\fid{F-12}}, \hyperref[m:M-6]{M-6}), defeats every SE row of the matrix. There is no field update path, so it stays until the unit is regressed and reflashed. A leaked \code{OEM2KEY} lets its holder regress to RDP~1 and then to RDP~0.5, which erases and replaces the NonSecure image while Secure flash and page~22 stay. Such an image can proxy USB, arm modes, and observe ciphertext, but cannot read Secure RAM or flash. A leaked \code{OEM1KEY} allows a mass erase, a denial of service. A lost \code{OEM2KEY} makes RDP~2 permanent.
\end{itemize}
\end{description}

\subsection{Physical, fault-injection, side-channel, and cold-boot threats (SE)}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Debug and readout.} At RDP~2, SWD and the system bootloader are disabled and the option bytes are frozen. The only debug access left is the \code{OEM2KEY} request under reset, one attempt per power cycle against a 64-bit key. Reading page~22 then requires an invasive attack: decapsulation and microprobing, or a published RDP downgrade or fault for this silicon family. Page~22 holds plaintext device secrets (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}). Pads additionally need TROPIC01, \code{fill_id}, and the PIN.
\item \textbf{Fault injection (voltage, clock, or EM glitch).} The software decisions a glitch could target are:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item the owner-pin result in \code{tls_app_*_enter} (a single branch on the return of \code{se_tls_user_pin_peer});
  \item \code{se_nv_has_owner()} in \code{se_auth_begin_owner};
  \item the time-floor branch;
  \item the cursor advance in \code{se_tropic_OTP_xor_consume}.
  \end{itemize}
  The cursor advance is checked twice: consume reads both cursor copies again before the advance and reads them back after it (\S\ref{sec:sca}, \hyperref[c:C-7]{C-7}). The other three are single branches without control-flow integrity counters. This is an accepted residual: the PIN itself is enforced inside TROPIC01 by M\&D, so an MCU glitch does not bypass the PIN budget, and a glitched owner pin still needs the PIN to open pads. The MCU has no glitch detectors comparable to TROPIC01's.
\item \textbf{Side channels.} AES-GCM, SHA-2/3, HMAC, ML-KEM, and ML-DSA all run in software on the M33, because the U535 has no hardware AES or PKA. wolfSSL constant-time options are in use (bitsliced AES, 4-bit GCM table), and the owner-pin comparison is constant-time. For an attacker holding a stolen SE, the most exposed computations to power or EM analysis are ML-DSA signing in the device handshake and ML-KEM decapsulation. This is a residual. For ML-DSA it is reduced by building the firmware with the wolfSSL release that adds side-channel protection, once that release is available (\S\ref{sec:sca}).
\item \textbf{Cold boot and SRAM remanence.} The SE uses on-chip SRAM, not DRAM. Remanence after a power cut is short at room temperature, but not zero. Session secrets (\code{final_key}, the exporter, TLS keys) are wiped on close and abort. A private rewrite of page~22 also copies \code{secure_dwk} into the SRAM4 work buffer until that buffer is wiped. \code{PROVISION} does not load it (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}). After a sudden power loss, SRAM is cleared on the next start: the option bytes \code{SRAM_RST=0} and \code{SRAM2_RST=0} make the hardware erase it on reset, \code{Reset_Handler} zeroes Secure SRAM and SRAM4 before any C code runs, and the internal tampers erase SRAM2 (\hyperref[c:C-8]{C-8}). An image booted from the device therefore cannot read earlier contents. What is left is an attacker who keeps the chip powered down and reads the cells by an invasive path. This is an accepted low residual.
\item \textbf{Board-level SPI.} The bus runs over the devkit's on-board traces, which cannot be moved or covered by a board change (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}). Under model~B, an attacker who recorded \code{PAIRING} decrypts the L3 traffic and opens their own sessions. A recording of later sessions alone does not suffice, because the pairing public key is never on the bus outside \code{PAIRING}. The PIN itself is not on the bus: the MCU sends HMAC outputs that include an MCU-only pepper. Without L3 access, brute force needs live M\&D slots, of which there are 7 tries. With L3 access and a recorded successful PIN entry, the budget can be reset, which leaves the PIN's entropy and the owner key as the bounds (\hyperref[m:M-3]{M-3}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Side-channel and fault-injection analysis in depth}\label{sec:sca}
This subsection expands the summary above. It is based on the build configuration (\code{Secure/wolfSSL/user_settings.h}), the PIN code (\code{se_tropic_pin.c}), and the OTP code (\code{se_tropic_otp_xor.c}, \code{se_tropic_cursor.c}). Every statement is about exposure, not measured leakage (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Attacker preconditions.} Every attack in this subsection needs the physical SE (model~A or~B) and, for profiled attacks, a second identical unit. At RDP~2 the attacker cannot load code, so they cannot trigger arbitrary operations. They can only drive the operations the USB protocol exposes: TLS handshakes (ML-KEM-768 encapsulation and ML-DSA-44 signing when a mode is armed), AES-256-GCM record processing, and PIN attempts. Unlimited ML-DSA traces therefore cost only USB reconnections. Unlimited PIN-path traces do \emph{not} exist, because each attempt consumes M\&D slots.

\paragraph{AES-256-GCM (software, bitsliced).} \code{WC_AES_BITSLICED} with \code{WC_AES_BS_WORD_SIZE 16} replaces table look-ups with a boolean circuit. This removes cache-timing and table-index leakage. The M33 in the U535 has no data cache on SRAM, so cache timing is a minor concern anyway. GHASH uses \code{GCM_TABLE_4BIT} (\code{WOLF_CONF_AESGCM 2}), which avoids the bit-dependent branch on $H$, but the 4-bit table is indexed by data XOR $H$. Bitslicing does \emph{not} defeat power or EM correlation analysis (CPA): the circuit still processes key-dependent intermediate values, and there is no masking or shuffling. The AES keys involved are per-session TLS traffic keys and the OTP-session keys derived from \code{final_key}. A CPA attacker must recover a key within one session, and that key is worth only that session's records. The pads themselves are never AES-processed by a fixed long-term key on the MCU. \textbf{Exposure: low to moderate} (session keys only).

\paragraph{ML-DSA-44 signing (device identity).} Signing uses \code{WOLFSSL_MLDSA_SIGN_SMALL_MEM}; ML-DSA-65 and ML-DSA-87 are disabled. The device identity key is long-term and is used in every handshake where the device authenticates, so the attacker controls the number of traces. Published attacks on unmasked Dilithium/ML-DSA implementations (CPA or template attacks on the $c\cdot s_1$ multiplication and the NTT, and fault attacks on the rejection loop and on nonce or $y$ reuse) apply to unprotected software on Cortex-M4/M33-class cores. The wolfSSL release in use provides no masked ML-DSA. wolfSSL has confirmed to us that side-channel protection for ML-DSA will be added in a later release. Recovering $s_1$ lets an attacker impersonate \emph{this} device to SAEs and to the owner's client. It does \emph{not} yield pads, which are in TROPIC01 behind the PIN. \textbf{Exposure: moderate} (identity impersonation of one unit; mitigated operationally by revocation at the CA, \S\ref{sec:ca}). \textbf{Countermeasure:} once the wolfSSL release with ML-DSA side-channel protection is available, we build the firmware with it. New units are flashed with that build at manufacturing. An issued unit receives it only through the \code{OEM2KEY} regression path: the key holder regresses the unit and reflashes it, which erases the device secrets, so the unit is enrolled again (\hyperref[ta:TA-9]{TA-9}).

\paragraph{ML-KEM-768 (TLS key exchange).} The SE is the TLS client, so it performs key generation and \emph{decapsulation} with a fresh ephemeral key per handshake. Single-trace attacks on the NTT or on message encoding, as published for unprotected Kyber, need a profiled template and work per session. They recover that session's shared secret, but there is no long-term ML-KEM key to steal. The ML-KEM-768 used inside \code{PROVISION} (\code{kem_ct} in the downlink) is likewise ephemeral. Implicit rejection in wolfSSL removes the timing oracle of the FO transform. \textbf{Exposure: low} (per-session only).

\paragraph{SHA-2/3 and HMAC (PIN KDF, HKDF).} \code{pin_hmac_sha384} processes the PIN-derived \code{kdf_in} and the MCU pepper. These computations run only during a PIN attempt, which is budget-limited, so the attacker gets at most 7 traces per enrollment, and trace collection itself burns attempts. A template attack within 7 traces on HMAC-SHA-384 is at the research frontier. The pepper (page~22) is the higher-value target, and it is more simply obtained by readout (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}).

\paragraph{Timing.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The owner SPKI comparison is constant-time (\code{se_tls_user.c}).
\item The PIN tag check is constant-time: \code{pin_ct_equal(nvm.t, t_, sizeof(t_))} (\code{se_tropic_pin.c} line~328; helper at line~137) OR-accumulates over all 48 bytes through a volatile accumulator, with no early exit. In any case $t$ is a 48-byte HMAC over the M\&D output $s$. A wrong PIN yields an unrelated $s$, so no partial match could be steered, and every attempt has already consumed its slots.
\item ML-DSA signing time varies with the number of rejection rounds. This is inherent to the scheme and leaks nothing about the key beyond what is public.
\item USB response latency for PIN success versus failure is distinguishable, but the result is returned explicitly anyway.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Glitching the PIN check.} The order in \code{se_tropic_pin_check} is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item read the sealed NVM blob from TROPIC01 R-MEM slot~511;
\item decrement the counter \code{nvm.i} and write it back \emph{before} any PIN-dependent computation (lines 289--294);
\item run M\&D on slots $2i$, $2i{+}1$;
\item check the tag;
\item on success only, re-arm slots $i..6$ and restore the counter.
\end{enumerate}
A glitch that skips the counter write-back (step~2) does not give a free attempt. The M\&D slot pair $i$ is destroyed \emph{inside TROPIC01} by the wrong-PIN MAC, so retrying at the same $i$ yields a garbage $s$ even for the correct PIN. In effect the budget is enforced by the TROPIC01 slot state, not by the MCU counter. A glitch that forces the tag check to pass (step~4) yields a wrong $s$, so \code{final_key} is wrong and the OTP-session state cannot be decrypted. The attacker gains nothing but a destroyed pair. A glitch during re-arm (step~5) after a \emph{correct} PIN can only leave the device with fewer tries. \textbf{The PIN budget is robust against MCU glitches.} Attacking it needs faults \emph{inside} TROPIC01, whose vendor documentation claims voltage, laser, temperature, and EM glitch sensors with active shielding (\hyperref[ta:TA-1]{TA-1}).

\paragraph{Glitching RDP~2 and boot.} RDP~2 is enforced by option-byte loading in the boot ROM. Published voltage-glitch attacks have downgraded or bypassed RDP on older STM32 families (F0, F1, F3, F4, L0, L4). No public, reproducible RDP~2 bypass for the STM32U5 is known to us. Such a bypass is plausible for a well-funded model~B attacker, and it is the precondition for the page-22 readout (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}). Because there is no field update, a silicon bypass cannot be answered by a patch, and the risk is accepted as a residual. BOOT\_LOCK pins the boot address, which narrows the glitch window to the option-byte load.

\paragraph{Glitching Secure decisions.} The highest-impact target is consume in \code{se_tropic_otp_xor.c}, because a skipped cursor advance could cause \emph{keystream reuse}. Consume runs in this order:
\begin{enumerate}
\item read both cursor copies (TROPIC01 mcounter and MCU NV) and require them to match (\code{se_tropic_qkd_cursor_get});
\item read and decrypt the pad blob;
\item read both cursors again: they must still name the slot just decrypted, otherwise the pad buffer is wiped and consume returns \code{DEVICE_TAMPERED} (lines 158--168);
\item advance the cursor, TROPIC01 counter first, then MCU NV (line~171, \code{se_tropic_qkd_cursor_advance_from_slot});
\item read both copies back and require each to equal the new value (\code{qkd_cursor_verify}, \code{se_tropic_cursor.c} lines 48--67 and 109; also after the exhausted marker, line~80);
\item erase the pad's R-MEM slot (line~177);
\item release the XOR result (line~184).
\end{enumerate}
A single fault on the advance write is caught by the read-back, and a fault on the first cursor read is caught by the second. Keystream reuse needs the attacker to suppress the advance, its verification, and the erase in one consume, and a later consume to read the same, still-present slot. A glitch on the erase alone leaves an orphan slot behind the cursor, which is not reachable. The attacker also needs the PIN, because consume runs only inside an authenticated \code{ENCRYPT}. An attacker who holds the PIN can already read pads legitimately, so the residual case is producing two ciphertexts under one pad \emph{intended} for a victim, which needs several coordinated faults.

The owner-pin branch in \code{tls_app_*_enter}, \code{se_nv_has_owner()} in \code{se_auth_begin_owner}, and the time-floor branch are single decisions without duplicated checks or random delays. This is an accepted residual. A glitched owner pin still needs the PIN for pads. A glitched \code{se_nv_has_owner()} gives at most the effect of \hyperref[f:F-05]{\fid{F-05}}, a replaced SAE CA, which pads still need the PIN to exploit. A glitched time floor only lets a session arm on an older time.

\paragraph{TROPIC01 counters and slots.} Two TROPIC01 mechanisms protect the pads: the M\&D slots, which enforce the PIN budget in hardware, and the pad R-MEM slots, which are erased after use. The pad cursor is \emph{not} hardware-monotonic. The firmware writes it with \code{lt_mcounter_init(h, ctr, next)} on every advance (\code{se_tropic_cursor.c} line~100), and \code{se_tropic_qkd_arm_halves} writes it again at each new fill (\code{secure_qkd_ingest.c} line~117, after all pad slots were erased in \code{se_tropic_qkd_provision_begin}). Its value is therefore protected only by firmware logic and by the cross-check against MCU NV in \code{se_tropic_qkd_cursor_get} and the read-back after every write, which return \code{DEVICE_TAMPERED} on a mismatch. A rewound counter alone gives no reuse, because the slots it would point back to are already erased. Pad uniqueness rests on \emph{erase after use}, not on the counter. Erasing the TROPIC01 pairing does not restore erased slots.

\paragraph{Cold boot and SRAM remanence.} The relevant secrets in Secure SRAM are TLS traffic keys, the exporter, \code{final_key}, the OTP-session state, the L3 session keys, pad chunks during XOR, and \code{secure_dwk} for the duration of a private full-page rewrite of page~22. \code{PROVISION} appends to the blank tail and does not stage \code{secure_dwk}. Session secrets are wiped with \code{wc_ForceZero} on close and abort. The NV work buffer is wiped when the commit finishes. Brown-out and power removal skip these wipes, so SRAM is cleared on the next start instead, in three layers (\hyperref[c:C-8]{C-8}). First, the option bytes \code{SRAM_RST=0} and \code{SRAM2_RST=0} make the hardware erase SRAM1, SRAM4, and SRAM2 on every system reset. Second, \code{Reset_Handler} zeroes the whole Secure RAM range (SRAM1 and the Secure part of SRAM2, \code{0x30000000}--\code{0x30038000}) and all of SRAM4 before \code{SystemInit}, using registers only. This layer does not depend on the option bytes. Third, the TAMP internal tampers erase SRAM2, the backup registers, BKPSRAM, and the PKA SRAM while the device runs, on a backup-domain voltage or temperature excursion, a crypto peripheral fault, or a debug access at RDP level~2, and then reset the MCU. The Secure image checks \code{FLASH_OPTR} at boot and records a missing erase bit in \code{se_sram_erase_status}. It does not refuse to run, because at RDP~2 the option bytes are frozen and a refusal would brick the unit, while the software wipe already covers the gap. To read SRAM after power loss, an attacker must therefore keep the chip from booting and read the cells with an invasive path: decapsulation with probing, and in practice cooling, because remanence in 40\,nm-class SRAM at room temperature fades within milliseconds to seconds. A debug port is not available at RDP~2. \textbf{Exposure: low.} The residual is the invasive readout of a powered-down chip.

\paragraph{Debug.} At RDP~2, JTAG/SWD and the system bootloader are disabled. Only the holder of \code{OEM2KEY} can re-enable debug, by regressing to RDP~1, where flash and SRAM stay unreadable. The lab RDP~0 and RDP~1 configurations are not used in production (\S\ref{sec:context}). The TROPIC01 debug or maintenance interfaces are vendor-locked in production parts. Before the final lock, production units are checked by reading back the option bytes and both OEM key lock bits (parameter table, \S\ref{sec:params}).

\subsection{Firmware and memory threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No field update.} There is no field update path at RDP~2. A defect in the shipped image stays until the \code{OEM2KEY} holder regresses the unit. The NonSecure image can be replaced at RDP~0.5 with the secrets kept. Replacing the Secure image needs RDP~0 with \code{OEM1KEY}, a mass erase, and a new enrolment.
\item \textbf{Malicious NonSecure.} It cannot be installed at RDP~2 without \code{OEM2KEY} (\hyperref[ta:TA-9]{TA-9}). If one were present from the factory, it could proxy USB, arm modes, and observe ciphertext. It could not read Secure RAM or flash, because the SAU, the MPCBB, and the watermark block it.
\item \textbf{Memory safety.} The NSC, frame, MANAGE, and \code{OWNER SET} parsers bound every length by the packet and buffer sizes. Every NonSecure pointer passes \texttt{cmse\_check\_address\_range}, lengths are capped at \texttt{SECURE\_USB\_PKT\_MAX}, and RX data is copied once into the Secure ring, so there is no double fetch. No veneer returns key material.
\item \textbf{NSC entries.} The mutating commands are not NSC entries. NonSecure chooses the TLS mode and the Unix time, both bounded by \texttt{se\_tls\_arm} and \texttt{se\_time\_set\_unix}.
\item \textbf{GTZC and MPCBB lock.} The GTZC and MPCBB configuration is not locked (\code{MPCBB_LockConfig = 0}), and SRAM1 relies on its reset default. This is an accepted residual: changing it needs Secure code execution, which already has every secret.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cryptographic and logic threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pad reuse.} The cursor is advanced in the TROPIC01 mcounter and in MCU NV \emph{before} the pad is erased and used, and both copies are read again before the advance and read back after it. A mismatch returns \code{DEVICE_TAMPERED}. The pad HKDF includes \code{fill_id}. The encrypt and decrypt halves use disjoint ranges. A decrypt skip-ahead burns the skipped pads. The slot index comes from the unauthenticated ciphertext, so a forged ciphertext can burn pads (\hyperref[f:F-02]{\fid{F-02}}). There is no rewind path. A power cut during consume burns a pad, which is accepted.
\item \textbf{Pad reuse on the SAE.} Both rows of a share carry one lease token, and a node delivers stored material to the peer only when the token of its row matches the token of the counterpart row. Writes and deletes run under the pair lease of the caller (\hyperref[c:C-2]{C-2}), so their integrity depends on RMI integrity, which includes the handover path (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}). A device can hold keys after a failed commit, and the pair locks are lost when an SAE restarts.
\item \textbf{Zeroization.} \code{wc_ForceZero} is applied to the exporter, the uplink buffer, the MANAGE buffers, DER scratch, the pairing key copies, and the OTP session state. wolfSSL is re-initialised per session, with an arena reset. Wiping on power loss is impossible by nature.
\item \textbf{RNG.} The TRNG seeds Hash\_DRBG, and the seed function fails closed on HAL error. TROPIC01 has its own TRNG for its keys. Health-test behaviour depends on the HAL (\hyperref[ta:TA-1]{TA-1}).
\item \textbf{Time.} \code{se_time_set_unix} never lowers the NV floor. A stale value is clamped, and the session arms on the floor. This holds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{USB threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{BadUSB or rogue host.} The SE is a USB \emph{device} and exposes only CDC ACM (no DFU, MSC, or HID). A malicious host can send any frames. It can cause enumeration-level DoS, read console oracles (\hyperref[f:F-10]{\fid{F-10}}), and arm TLS modes. It cannot pass the owner pin or the PROVISION CA check. On an empty device it can win \code{OWNER SET}, because the first caller wins. That is acceptable because enrollment happens in the owner's hands.
\item \textbf{Malformed packets.} Magic, type, and length are validated. Lengths are capped by \code{USB_FRAME_MAX}, the 160-byte command line, and the 16~KiB and 8~KiB rings. Type \code{0x01} is dropped until a mode is armed. Type \code{0x00} during TLS is queued, not parsed as TLS.
\item \textbf{DMA races.} USB FS on the U535 uses its own packet memory, not bus-master DMA. NonSecure copies the data into its own buffer. The NSC veneer copies it once into the Secure ring after a CMSE range check. A NonSecure race on that buffer can corrupt only bytes NonSecure already controls. Lengths are register arguments, so there is no time-of-check/time-of-use on them. No DMA channel is configured that could target Secure memory.
\item \textbf{The SE as an attack platform.} The firmware cannot be changed at RDP~2 without the OEM keys, and the descriptors are CDC ACM only, so the SE cannot turn into a HID keyboard against the PC or the SAE.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{SAE and network threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RMI over an untrusted WAN.} With mTLS (\code{PURE_PQC}, \code{needClientAuth=true}) and SPKI pinning, an attacker without a \emph{pinned} leaf gets only handshake reachability (\hyperref[f:F-06]{\fid{F-06}}). A mis-issued \code{root-ca} leaf is disconnected before the first RMI byte. A compromised pinned peer is limited to its own records, the handover deletion (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}), and metadata reads (\hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}). Its return values pass the reply filter (\hyperref[c:C-4]{C-4}).
\item \textbf{KME.} See \hyperref[f:F-03]{\fid{F-03}}.
\item \textbf{Provisioning binding.} \code{SeSessionBinding.verify} checks the client hash $=$ H(TLS SPKI $\|$ ECC pub) and the signature over the TLS exporter; \code{SeConstants} fixes uplink v5 and downlink v3. The uplink is bound to this session and to the mTLS leaf, and the P-256 signature is not used as the post-quantum identity, so this holds under model~B.
\item \textbf{Local host.} SQLite is \code{0600}. Pad material is HSM-wrapped at rest. Pad plaintext is in process RAM during fetch and provisioning, which is accepted.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{USER (home PC)}
UserApp opens no listening socket and no outbound URL. It uses only USB CDC, and it is the TLS server on that stream. Household Wi-Fi therefore attacks the PC itself, not a UserApp port.

A thief who has the PC, the owner PKCS\#12, and its password can:
\begin{itemize}
\item run \code{MANAGE}, including \code{CREDS SAE} (\hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}) and \code{PAIRING LOAD} (\hyperref[f:F-09]{\fid{F-09}});
\item replace the SAE CA, and so act as a SAE towards this device and inject pads they know.
\end{itemize}
The same thief \textbf{cannot} run \code{ENCRYPT} or \code{DECRYPT} without the PIN, which is only typed and never stored. \code{pairing-key.hex} (\code{rw-------}) plus SPI access gives L3 as the paired host (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}).

\paragraph{Reading the matrix.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Pad \emph{confidentiality} rests on the SAE hosts and the KME link. Any compromise there reads pads in bulk, independent of the SE and the PIN.
\item Pad \emph{use on the device} rests on the conjunction of the owner key and the PIN. No single stolen item gives E/D.
\item Record \emph{integrity} between SAEs rests on the \emph{pinned} peers. Each writes only under a pair lease it holds (\hyperref[c:C-2]{C-2}), and can delete a share of another pair through the handover path (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}).
\item The cheapest DoS needs only the device: burn the PIN budget.
\item A forged ciphertext burns the decrypt pads if the user decrypts it (\hyperref[f:F-02]{\fid{F-02}}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Security controls that hold}\label{sec:controls}
These controls are implemented in the current source and covered by its tests. Paths are relative to \code{JAVA_APPS/modules/sae-node/src/main/java/fel/cvut/node/interNodeCommunication/} unless stated otherwise.
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.2em]
\item[C-1 RMI peer authentication by SPKI pin]\phantomsection\label{c:C-1}
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{SAE_NODES_FILE} names the SAE directory, a JSON list \code{[\{"saeId","name","location","hostname","port","spkiSha384"\}]} that every site holds an identical copy of. \code{SaeNodes} loads it and \code{KnownPeers} takes the pins from it. It is mandatory: \code{SaeNodes.fromEnvironment} (line~142) and \code{RmiManager.start} (line~114) fail closed without it.
\item File check: \code{SaeNodes.loadTrusted} (lines 124--139) resolves symlinks and refuses a target that is not a regular file or that is group- or other-writable (on POSIX file systems). Every field is required; \code{saeId}, \code{name}, \code{spkiSha384} and \code{hostname} with \code{port} must each be unique (lines 67--84).
\item Server side: the accepted socket is wrapped. After the \code{root-ca} mutual-TLS handshake, the first read resolves the peer leaf against the pins, or against the node's own leaf. An unpinned peer is disconnected before any RMI byte is parsed. Every read binds the resolved SAE ID as the caller of the dispatch thread (\code{RmiPeerPinning} lines 42--74 and 148--191; \code{RmiCallerContext}).
\item Client side: the server leaf must be pinned. If the directory names the endpoint, the leaf must belong to that SAE (\code{RmiManager} line~288).
\item Self-pin check: before RMI starts, the own entry's \code{spkiSha384} must equal the SPKI SHA-384 of the node's own leaf, so a swapped certificate or directory stops the node (\code{Node} lines 155 and 561--564; \code{SaeNodes.requireOwnPin} line~173).
\end{itemize}
\item[C-2 Caller-based authorization in \code{NodeCommandsService}]\phantomsection\label{c:C-2}
\begin{itemize}
\item Every call needs an authenticated caller (\code{requireCaller}, line~161).
\item \code{lockPair} (line~71) takes the lease in the caller's name. \code{renewPair} and \code{releasePair} (lines 82 and 90) act only on a lease that the caller holds with that token (\code{holdsAsCaller}, line~155).
\item \code{insert} (line~102) accepts a record only when its header \code{saeId} is the caller (line~106) and the caller holds a live lease with the given token (line~110). The KME fetch uses the authenticated ID. The row \code{KEYS_AVAILABLE} and its material are written in one transaction that checks the token, the expiry, and the issuer; otherwise the call throws \code{PairLockLostException}.
\item \code{deleteRecord} (line~140) is a compare-and-delete under the caller's lease: it removes the row only when it carries the shown token, and returns \code{DELETED}, \code{ABSENT}, or \code{TOKEN_MISMATCH}. Every deletion logs the caller.
\item The lease checks do not restrict \emph{which} pair a caller may lock, so a non-party can delete a share (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}); \code{getRecordMetadata} answers any caller (\hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}).
\end{itemize}
\item[C-3 Bind address]\phantomsection\label{c:C-3} The registry and the object port bind \code{NODE_RMI_BIND} (default: the own entry's \code{hostname}) through the 5-argument \code{SoftwareTls.createServerSocket} (\code{RmiManager} lines 34, 119--122, and 304--307).
\item[C-4 Deserialization allow-lists for RMI calls and replies]\phantomsection\label{c:C-4} Both directions of RMI pass JEP~290 filters (\code{RmiSerialFilter}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Call arguments.} The \code{NodeCommands} object is exported with the per-object filter \code{CALLS} (\code{RmiSerialFilter} lines 49--60 and 108; \code{RmiManager} lines 124--130). Its limits are \code{maxdepth=8}, \code{maxrefs=20000}, \code{maxarray=10000}, and \code{maxbytes=4194304}. Only \code{Address}, \code{ClientRecord} with its header and data, \code{String}, \code{Integer}/\code{Number}, and the JDK list implementations are allowed; everything else is rejected (\code{!*}).
\item \textbf{Return values and the registry stub.} \code{RmiManager.start} installs \code{REPLIES} as the process-wide filter before the first export (\code{RmiManager} line~118; \code{installForReplies}, \code{RmiSerialFilter} lines 120--130). It applies to everything a caller deserializes: the reply records and enums (\code{RecordMetadata}, \code{RecordAvailability}, \code{DeleteOutcome}), \code{java.time.Instant}, \code{String}, the boxed numbers, the JDK lists, and the RMI stub classes (lines 63--98). Exceptions are accepted only from \code{java.lang}, \code{java.io}, \code{java.rmi}, \code{java.rmi.server}, \code{java.sql}, and \code{fel.cvut} (lines 101--102 and 166--169). A dynamic proxy is accepted only when it implements \code{NodeCommands} or \code{java.rmi.Remote} and nothing else, which is the shape of the stub the registry returns (lines 149--164). The reply limits are \code{maxdepth=16} (an exception with its cause chain nests deeper than call arguments), \code{maxrefs=20000}, \code{maxarray=10000}, and \code{maxbytes=4194304}. Every rejection is logged with the class and the limits reached. A filter that is already set with \code{-Djdk.serialFilter} is kept and logged, because the process-wide filter can be set only once.
\item The registry keeps the JDK's built-in registry filter for the calls it receives.
\end{itemize}
\item[C-5 Secret prompt]\phantomsection\label{c:C-5} \code{fel/cvut/se/EnvSecrets.scan} (lines 51--64) reads a missing secret only with \code{Console.readPassword}, and only when \code{interactiveConsole()} (line~70) reports a real terminal; on JDK~22+ this uses \code{Console.isTerminal}. Otherwise it throws a clear \code{IllegalStateException} and never reads \code{System.in}.
\item[C-6 Constant-time PIN tag check (firmware)]\phantomsection\label{c:C-6} \code{se_tropic_pin.c} compares the 48-byte tag with \code{pin_ct_equal} (helper at line~137, use at line~328): a volatile OR-accumulate with no early exit.
\item[C-7 OTP cursor re-read and verify-after-write (firmware)]\phantomsection\label{c:C-7} Consume in \code{se_tropic_otp_xor.c} reads both cursor copies again after decrypting a pad and requires them to still name that slot (lines 158--168). \code{se_tropic_qkd_cursor_advance_from_slot} and the exhausted marker read both copies back after every write and compare them with the written value (\code{qkd_cursor_verify}, \code{se_tropic_cursor.c} lines 48--67, 80, and 109). Any difference wipes the pad buffer and returns \code{DEVICE_TAMPERED}, before the pad is erased or the XOR result is released.
\item[C-8 SRAM erase on reset and tamper (firmware and option bytes)]\phantomsection\label{c:C-8} \code{Reset_Handler} in \code{startup_stm32u535cctx.s} (lines 76--99) zeroes \code{_swipe_ram}..\code{_ewipe_ram} (all Secure RAM, including the stack) and \code{_swipe_sram4}..\code{_ewipe_sram4} (all of SRAM4) with \code{stmia} before \code{SystemInit}; the linker script defines these ranges from the memory regions and asserts that they are 16-byte multiples (\code{STM32U535CCTX_FLASH.ld} lines 48--53). The lockdown sets \code{SRAM_RST=0} and \code{SRAM2_RST=0} before RDP~2, and \code{se_tamper_init} reports either bit left at 1 in \code{se_sram_erase_status}. \code{se_tamper_init} (\code{se_tamper.c} lines 47--90) makes TAMP Secure-only, enables the backup-domain monitors, and arms ITAMP1, 2, and 9, plus ITAMP6 at RDP level~2, in confirmed mode with erase; \code{TAMP_IRQHandler} clears the flags and resets the MCU (\code{se_tamper_event}, lines 92--100). The host model has no reset or TAMP, so this control is checked in the linked Secure image (symbols, vector table, and disassembly of \code{Reset_Handler}), not by a host test.
\end{description}

\section{Findings}\label{sec:findings}
Each finding is a residual risk. It gives its context, the attacker model, the evidence (path, function, and lines), the attack sequence, the impact, and the scores, and it ends with the reason it is accepted or with its operational countermeasure (Section~\ref{sec:countermeasures}). The findings are sorted by severity and then by CVSS 3.1 score.

\subsection*{F-01: \texttt{CREDS SAE} replaces the PROVISION trust anchor with the owner key only \hfill \sev{High}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-01}
\textbf{Context:} locked SE; home PC compromise. \textbf{Model:} A. The attacker has stolen the owner key and its PKCS\#12 password, and has physical USB access to the SE.

\textbf{Evidence.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{se_manage_apply} accepts \code{SE_MANAGE_CREDS_SAE} only with \code{pin_len == 0}, so no PIN is involved.
\item \code{do_creds_sae} checks only the length (\code{se_manage.c} lines 252--264).
\item \code{se_creds_set_sae_ca} overwrites the CA and keeps the device certificate (\code{se_creds.c} lines 212--237). It does not compare with the previous CA, check \code{BasicConstraints}, or ask for confirmation.
\item The only authentication is the owner SPKI pin on the \code{MANAGE} TLS session.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Attack sequence.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Steal \code{user.p12} and its password from the home PC.
\item Connect the victim SE and run \code{MANAGE CREDS SAE} with the attacker's CA.
\item Run \code{PROVISION} against an attacker SAE whose leaf chains to that CA, injecting pads the attacker knows.
\item The victim later encrypts with known keystream.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{What the stolen owner key gives and does not give.}
\begin{itemize}
\item It gives the direct ability to \emph{spoof the SAE CA} on that device and \emph{inject pads}.
\item It allows the other \code{MANAGE} actions: \code{KEYGEN}, peer edits, \code{PAIRING}/\code{PAIRING LOAD}, and \code{INSERT SIGNED CSR}.
\item It does \textbf{not} allow \code{ENCRYPT} or \code{DECRYPT}. Those need the PIN, which TROPIC01 M\&D enforces; 7 failed tries destroy the KEK.
\item It does not grant RMI access, because the RMI truststore is \code{root-ca}, not the CA installed on the SE.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Accepted:} \code{CREDS SAE} stays owner-only by product decision. The owner is the one party who decides which SAE provisions the device, and the command never gives \code{ENCRYPT} or \code{DECRYPT}. The acceptance assumes the owner key is password-protected and the device stays with its user (\hyperref[ta:TA-8]{TA-8}).

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:L/PR:H/UI:R/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:L} $=$ \textbf{5.7}. \code{CVSS:4.0/AV:P/AC:L/AT:N/PR:H/UI:P/VC:H/VI:H/VA:L/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N} $=$ \textbf{6.9}. STRIDE: S, T, E.

\subsection*{F-02: Forged ciphertext exhausts the decrypt pads \hfill \sev{Medium}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-02}
\textbf{Context:} any channel that delivers a ciphertext to the user. \textbf{Model:} a network attacker who can send the user a message. The attacker has no key, no PIN, and no access to the device.

\textbf{Evidence.} A ciphertext is the \code{ENCRYPT} reply: \code{n_pads}, then one record per pad with \code{u16 slot}, \code{u16 chunk_len}, and the ciphertext chunk (\code{secure_otp.c} lines 36--39 and 253--258). The slot index is taken from the record and is not authenticated. On \code{DECRYPT}, \code{se_tropic_otp_xor_pad} (\code{se_tropic_otp_xor.c} lines 394--474) accepts any slot of the decrypt half that lies between the cursor and the end of the last fill (lines 435--457). It then moves the cursor to that slot with \code{se_tropic_qkd_cursor_skip_one}, which erases every pad it passes (\code{se_tropic_cursor.c} lines 540--554). \code{se_tropic_otp_xor_open} checks only that \code{n_pads} pads are available from the cursor (lines 360--375). A single record with \code{n_pads}~$=1$ and a slot near the end of the fill therefore burns all remaining decrypt pads. \code{se_tropic_OTP_xor_consume} XORs the chunk with the pad and returns the result (line 184). The ciphertext carries no integrity tag, so the forgery is not detected and the user gets garbage plaintext. UserApp passes the pasted hex to the device unchanged (\code{UserApplication.java} line 226, \code{ChipService.java} lines 34--38).

\textbf{Attack.} The attacker sends the user a crafted ciphertext over the channel the two users exchange ciphertexts on. The user runs \code{DECRYPT} on it in their own owner-pinned session with their own PIN. The attacker needs neither the owner key nor the PIN, only a user who decrypts the message.

\textbf{Impact.} D: one forged message burns the decrypt pads up to the chosen slot, at most the whole decrypt half. Pads are never rewound, so later ciphertexts from the peer for the burned slots fail on this device (slot behind the cursor, lines 462--463) until both devices are provisioned again. A forged record that names the slot of a genuine message in transit burns that pad before the genuine message arrives. T: the one-time pad is malleable. An attacker who intercepts a genuine ciphertext can flip chosen plaintext bits without detection, and with a known or guessed plaintext can replace it by any message of the same length. Confidentiality is not affected.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:R/S:U/C:N/I:L/A:H} $=$ \textbf{7.1}. \code{CVSS:4.0/AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:N/UI:A/VC:N/VI:L/VA:H/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N} $=$ \textbf{7.0}. STRIDE: D, T. The severity is Medium because the user must actively decrypt the message with the PIN, and a new provisioning restores the pads. \textbf{Countermeasure:} \hyperref[m:M-1]{M-1}.

\subsection*{F-03: SAE--KME hop is CLASSICAL TLS \hfill \sev{Medium (model B)}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-03}
\textbf{Evidence.} \code{Node.loadQkdClient} uses \code{SoftwareTls.TlsProfile.CLASSICAL}, and pad plaintext is carried over that link.

\textbf{Impact.} A model-B attacker who records the link and later breaks X25519 learns the pads. This does not break device \code{PROVISION} TLS.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:A/AC:H/PR:N/UI:N/S:C/C:H/I:N/A:N} $=$ \textbf{6.1}. CVSS:4.0 $=$ \textbf{6.0}. STRIDE: I. \textbf{Countermeasure:} \hyperref[m:M-2]{M-2}.

\subsection*{F-04: TROPIC01 L3 session is X25519 only; a recorded \texttt{PAIRING} gives L3 access \hfill \sev{Medium (model B)}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-04}
\textbf{Context:} locked SE (RDP~2) after \code{PAIRING}. \textbf{Model:} B, with physical access to the device.

\textbf{Exposed link.} The SE is the TS13 TROPIC01 USB devkit: the STM32U535 and a TROPIC01 soldered on one board. Our firmware replaces the devkit's stock USB-to-SPI bridge, so a USB host has no path to the bus; SPI1 and its GPIOs are Secure (\code{MX_GTZC_S_Init}). The MCU drives TROPIC01 over on-board traces: PA4 chip select, PA5 SCK, PA6 MISO, PA7 MOSI, PA0 TROPIC01 power, and PB0 GPO (\code{docs/HARDWARE.md} lines 127--132). An attacker who holds the devkit can probe these traces, or lift the TROPIC01 and drive it from their own SPI master. The board layout cannot be changed.

\textbf{Evidence.}
\begin{itemize}
\item L3 is Noise\_KK1\_25519\_AESGCM\_SHA256 (\code{libtropic_l3.c} line~86). The session keys come from three X25519 terms through HKDF: $\mathrm{DH}(e_H, E_T)$, $\mathrm{DH}(s_H, E_T)$, and $\mathrm{DH}(e_H, S_T)$ (lines 217--248). No term is post-quantum.
\item The bus carries the host ephemeral key $E_H$ and the pairing slot index in the handshake request, and the chip ephemeral key $E_T$ in the reply (lines 32--66). The host pairing public key $S_H$ is never sent in a handshake. The chip key $S_T$ is read from the chip's certificate store, and the certificate chain is not verified (\code{se_tropic.c} lines 158--161).
\item \code{PAIRING} writes the new $S_H$ with \code{lt_pairing_key_write} inside a session opened with the published SH0 key (\code{se_tropic.c} lines 436--442; \code{libtropic_user_config.h}), and then invalidates slot~0 (line~460).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What a model-B attacker gets.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{From recorded sessions after \code{PAIRING}: nothing beyond model~A.} Breaking $E_H$ gives the first and third terms, but the second needs $s_H$ or $S_H$, which the bus does not carry. This follows from the key schedule.
\item \textbf{From a recording of \code{PAIRING}: full L3 access.} The SH0 private key is public, so the attacker computes all three terms for that session, decrypts it, and reads $S_H$ from the \code{lt_pairing_key_write} payload. Breaking $S_H$ gives $s_H$. From then on the attacker decrypts every recorded session of this device and opens their own L3 sessions as the MCU. $s_H$ is also reachable without model~B, from page~22 (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}) or from \code{pairing-key.hex} on the home PC (\hyperref[ta:TA-8]{TA-8}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Impact of an L3 session.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pads, PIN, and device secrets stay confidential.} Pad blobs are AES-256-GCM under keys derived from the ML-KEM shared secret, \code{fill_id}, and the slot (\code{se_tropic_aead.c} lines 176--195). The ML-KEM seed is wrapped under a KEK derived from the PIN result and \code{secure_dwk} (\code{se_tropic_mlkem.c} lines 25--36). The PIN state is sealed under an MCU key (\code{se_tropic_pin.c} line~287). The M\&D inputs are HMACs over the PIN and an MCU-only pepper (lines 14--24 and 63--101), so neither recorded nor fresh traffic reveals the PIN or lets the attacker compute a valid M\&D input.
\item \textbf{No pad reuse.} A rewritten cursor mcounter or R-MEM slot fails the cross-check with the MCU NV cursor or the read-back, and the device returns \code{DEVICE_TAMPERED} (\hyperref[c:C-7]{C-7}). The MCU NV cursor is authoritative and out of the attacker's reach.
\item \textbf{The PIN budget can be reset.} The re-arm value $u$ is the same for every successful PIN check and crosses the bus inside L3 (\code{se_tropic_pin.c} lines 203--224 and 335--341). The sealed PIN state is bound only to its slot (\code{se_tropic_aead.c} lines 269--300), so an older copy is accepted again. An attacker who recorded one successful PIN entry, with a tap on the bus while the owner uses the device, can therefore re-arm the M\&D slots and restore the PIN state after every wrong guess. The guesses still run through the MCU inside an owner-pinned \code{ENCRYPT} or \code{DECRYPT} session, so they also need the owner key, and each costs one TLS handshake. The bound becomes the PIN's entropy instead of 7 tries. This follows from the M\&D call sequence; we have not run it against a chip.
\item \textbf{Denial of service.} Erasing R-MEM slots or the PIN state, burning M\&D slots, or rewriting the cursor counter leaves the device \code{DEVICE_TAMPERED} or without a PIN state until it is provisioned or enrolled again. Erasing the ECC key changes the client hash (\code{se_tropic.c} lines 370--376). Invalidating every pairing slot disables L3 for good. Model~A already has DoS by destroying the board or burning the PIN budget over USB.
\item \textbf{Bus MITM without $s_H$} cannot inject L3 commands, because they are AES-GCM protected. It can drop or corrupt frames or cut the TROPIC01 power line, which is DoS. The L2 requests that need no session (\code{Get_Info}, \code{Startup}, \code{Sleep}, \code{Get_Log}; \code{lt_l2_api_structs.h}) are open to model~A as well.
\item \textbf{ECDSA with the TROPIC01 ECC key} adds nothing: model~B derives the P-256 private key from the public key anyway, and the uplink binding does not rely on the signature as a post-quantum identity.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:H/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:L/A:H} $=$ \textbf{5.3}. \code{CVSS:4.0/AV:P/AC:H/AT:P/PR:N/UI:N/VC:L/VI:L/VA:H/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N} $=$ \textbf{4.4}. STRIDE: S, T, I, D. The severity is Medium because TROPIC01's whole command surface is exposed to anyone who recorded \code{PAIRING}, and the PIN budget then rests on the owner key and the PIN's entropy. Pads, the PIN, and device secrets are not disclosed.

\textbf{Treatment.} The SE is a stock devkit and the device protocol is fixed, so the bus cannot be rerouted, covered, or protected below L3. The exposure is controlled operationally (\hyperref[m:M-3]{M-3}): \code{PAIRING} runs in operator custody before issue, so no recording of the SH0 session exists; the devkit is sealed; and it stays with its user. \textbf{Accepted under model~B:} physical possession is the only control on the bus. An attacker who defeats it at \code{PAIRING} gains L3 access, but not pads, the PIN, or device secrets.

\subsection*{F-05: \texttt{OWNER SET} retry path installs the SAE CA without owner authentication \hfill \sev{Low}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-05}
\textbf{Context:} SE after a failed enrollment. \textbf{Model:} A, physical USB.

\textbf{Evidence.} \code{se_auth_begin_owner} refuses only when \code{se_nv_has_owner()} \emph{and} \code{se_nv_has_device_sk()} are both true (\code{se_auth.c} lines 42--45). \code{run_owner_set} skips \code{se_owner_set} when an owner exists, but still runs \code{se_device_id_ensure} and \code{se_creds_set_sae_ca} with the CA from the \emph{unsigned} blob (lines 70--91).

\textbf{Attack sequence.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The owner SPKI is written, but ML-DSA keygen fails.
\item A host that is not the owner sends \code{OWNER SET} with its own CA.
\item The device keeps the legitimate owner but now trusts the attacker's SAE CA.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Accepted:} the window exists only after a failed enrollment, and a failed \code{INIT} is visible to the user. The enrollment check in the parameter sheet (Section~\ref{sec:params}) confirms that \code{OWNER SET} answered ``ok'' and that the CSR exists before a device is issued, which closes the window. The retry path itself is what lets a legitimate owner finish an interrupted enrollment.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:H/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:N} $=$ \textbf{5.7}. \code{CVSS:4.0/AV:P/AC:L/AT:P/PR:N/UI:N/VC:H/VI:H/VA:N/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N} $=$ \textbf{5.3}. STRIDE: S, T.

\subsection*{F-06: RMI TLS handshake reachable by unauthenticated hosts on the bind address \hfill \sev{Low}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-06}
\textbf{Context:} production SAE. \textbf{Model:} A, any host that can reach the bind address.

\textbf{Evidence.} The registry and the object port bind \code{NODE_RMI_BIND}, which defaults to the own entry's \code{hostname} (\code{RmiManager} lines 118--121 and 304--306). The SPKI pin check runs after the mutual-TLS handshake, on the first read of the accepted socket (\code{RmiPeerPinning} lines 148--191). Source filtering and handshake rate limiting are left to the host firewall.

\textbf{Impact.} Any host that can route to the bind address can trigger the ML-DSA/ML-KEM handshake. Handshake floods are a DoS vector, and the pre-authentication TLS stack is exposed. An unpinned client gets no RMI access.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:N/I:N/A:L} $=$ \textbf{5.3}. \code{CVSS:4.0/AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:N/UI:N/VC:N/VI:N/VA:L/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N} $=$ \textbf{6.9}. STRIDE: D. The severity is Low, because the parameter sheet (Section~\ref{sec:params}) requires a firewall allow-list. \textbf{Countermeasure:} \hyperref[m:M-4]{M-4}.

\subsection*{F-07: RMI \texttt{deleteRecord} lets a pinned non-party delete a share under its own lease \hfill \sev{Low}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-07}
\textbf{Context:} production SAE; untrusted WAN. \textbf{Model:} A, holding a \emph{pinned} SAE leaf (a compromised peer SAE).

\textbf{Evidence.} \code{NodeCommandsService.lockPair} (line~71) takes a pair lease for any authenticated caller, and \code{deleteRecord} (lines 140--153) removes a row under that lease whenever the shown token matches; neither checks that the caller is a party of the row. \code{getRecordMetadata} returns the token (\hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}). The deletion is remote-first: the caller locks both nodes, lower SAE ID first, deletes the counterpart row, and then the origin row. Each deletion is logged with the caller's SAE ID (line~144). This path serves the re-provision handover: the device has moved to the caller's SAE, which must remove the old origin--peer share it is not a party to.

\textbf{Attack sequence.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compromise one pinned SAE.
\item Enumerate pairs with \code{getRecordMetadata} (\hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}).
\item Take \code{lockPair} on both nodes of each share, lower SAE ID first, and call \code{deleteRecord} with the token read in step~2, remote first. Both rows are deleted.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Impact.} Availability of pad distribution. Affected devices must be re-provisioned. No pad material is disclosed, and every such deletion is logged with the caller's SAE ID.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:H/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:L/A:L} $=$ \textbf{4.7}. \code{CVSS:4.0/AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:H/UI:N/VC:L/VI:L/VA:L/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N} $=$ \textbf{5.1}. STRIDE: T, D. The severity is Low in context, because it needs a pinned SAE key and only forces a re-provision.

\textbf{Accepted:} the re-provision handover needs this path, because the SAE the device has moved to must remove an old share it is not a party to. Only SAEs pinned in the SAE directory reach RMI (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}), and every deletion is logged with the caller's SAE ID.

\subsection*{F-08: SE does not validate the peer chain on ENCRYPT/DECRYPT/MANAGE \hfill \sev{Low}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-08}
\textbf{Evidence.} \code{tls_accept_peer_leaf} returns~1 and ignores \code{preverify}. Identity is the SPKI pin alone (\code{se_tls_user.c}). Validity period, issuer, and revocation of the owner certificate are never evaluated.

\textbf{Impact.} Revoking the owner key at the CA has no effect on the SE. A stolen owner key stays valid until \code{OWNER REPLACE}. For a lost owner key, the owner runs \code{OWNER REPLACE} with a new key.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:H/PR:H/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:L/A:N} $=$ \textbf{2.7}. CVSS:4.0 $=$ \textbf{1.0}. STRIDE: S.

\textbf{Accepted:} pinning by design. The SE trusts exactly one owner key, which is stronger than CA validation and needs no clock, CRL, or network on the device.

\subsection*{F-09: \texttt{PAIRING LOAD} is weaker than \texttt{PAIRING} \hfill \sev{Low}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-09}
\textbf{Evidence.} \code{do_pairing_load} requires only an empty NV pairing record and a valid format (\code{se_manage.c} lines 325--349). It then loads a caller-supplied X25519 key and slot. By contrast, \code{PAIRING} generates the key on the device and burns SH0.

\textbf{Sequence.} With the owner key and \code{pairing-key.hex}, on an SE whose NV pairing record is empty, an attacker restores the old pairing and speaks L3 again. Combined with SPI access, this exposes R-MEM ciphertext. Pads still need \code{secure_dwk}, \code{fill_id}, and the PIN.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:H/PR:H/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:L/A:N} $=$ \textbf{2.7}. CVSS:4.0 $=$ \textbf{1.0}. STRIDE: E.

\textbf{Accepted:} the command needs the owner key, \code{pairing-key.hex} from the owner's PC, and an empty NV pairing record at the same time, so it restores only a pairing the owner already holds. Even then, pads still need \code{secure_dwk}, \code{fill_id}, and the PIN.

\subsection*{F-10: Unauthenticated USB console discloses state \hfill \sev{Low}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-10}
\textbf{Evidence.} The SE-L1 commands (\code{PEER LIST}, \code{CLIENT HASH}/\code{CSR}, \code{TROPIC PUB}, \code{KEM PUB}, \code{OTP STATUS}) answer any host. Together they reveal enrollment state, peer nicknames and hashes, and the remaining pad budget.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N} $=$ \textbf{2.4}. CVSS:4.0 $=$ \textbf{2.4}. STRIDE: I.

\textbf{Accepted:} the read-only console is needed before any session exists. For example, \code{PEER CARD} in UserApp reads \code{CLIENT HASH} and \code{TROPIC PAD LEN} to build the card the users exchange (\hyperref[ta:TA-7]{TA-7}). The console answers never include key material, the PIN, or pads.

\subsection*{F-11: Plaintext device secrets in flash page 22; HDP not used \hfill \sev{Info (residual at RDP 2)}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-11}
\textbf{Evidence.} Page~22 holds \code{secure_dwk}, the ML-DSA-44 SK, and the pairing SK in plaintext (\code{se_nv.c}). \code{HDP1EN} is \code{0x0}. At RDP~2, reading them needs an invasive attack. With page 22 an attacker can impersonate the device in TLS. Pads still require TROPIC01 and the PIN.

A full-page rewrite copies \code{secure_dwk} into the SRAM4 work buffer for the erase and program, then wipes that buffer. That rewrite is the private path: an \code{ENCRYPT} or \code{DECRYPT} cursor commit, and owner, pairing, or peer updates. \code{PROVISION} does not erase the page. It appends four records (fill, arm, pad length, end cursors; 96~bytes) into the blank tail after byte 7344 and leaves \code{secure_dwk} in flash. The tail holds eight provisions. A ninth is refused and does not erase the page. \code{SE_OK} is sent only after the end-cursor slot has been programmed and read back. The next private rewrite checks the replayed cursors against the TROPIC01 counters, folds a match into the base record, and clears the tail. A mismatch leaves the page unchanged.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:H/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:N} $=$ \textbf{5.7}. CVSS:4.0 $=$ \textbf{5.3}. STRIDE: I. \textbf{Countermeasure:} \hyperref[m:M-5]{M-5}.

\subsection*{F-12: Factory SH0 is the L3 key until \texttt{PAIRING} \hfill \sev{Info}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-12}
\textbf{Evidence.} \code{libtropic_user_config.h} selects the published \code{lt_sh0priv_prod0} key. Before \code{PAIRING}, anyone with SPI access can open an L3 session. \textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:P/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:L/A:N} $=$ 3.5; CVSS:4.0 $=$ 1.0. \textbf{Countermeasure:} \hyperref[m:M-6]{M-6}.

\subsection*{F-13: PKI hygiene of the lab CA tooling \hfill \sev{Info} (lab-only)}\phantomsection\label{f:F-13}
\textbf{Scope.} \code{CertGenerator} and the two CAs it creates (\code{root-ca}, \code{client_ca}) are \textbf{lab-only} tooling for development and the test bench. They are not the production PKI. The observations below describe the lab tooling. Production requirements are in Section~\ref{sec:mit-ca}.

\textbf{Evidence (\code{CertGenerator}).}
\begin{itemize}
\item Node and device leaves have no \code{KeyUsage} or \code{ExtendedKeyUsage} (\code{issuePurePqcFromPublicKey}).
\item The CAs use serial~1 and a one-year validity, with no path-length constraint.
\item Both CA keys are created on one host under the same \code{pkcs12Password}.
\item The SE does not check that the \code{CREDS SAE} DER is a CA certificate; the owner chooses that certificate (\hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Impact.} In the lab, role separation rests on the issuer (and, for RMI, also on the SPKI pins, \hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}), and a single host compromise yields both CAs. A production deployment must not reuse this tooling.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:H/PR:H/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:L/A:N} $=$ \textbf{3.3}. CVSS:4.0 $=$ \textbf{2.1}. STRIDE: S, E. The scores apply only if the lab CAs were used in production. \textbf{Countermeasure:} \hyperref[m:M-7]{M-7}, a production CA as specified in Section~\ref{sec:mit-ca}.

\subsection*{F-14: RMI \texttt{getRecordMetadata} answers any pinned peer for any pair \hfill \sev{Info}}\phantomsection\label{f:F-14}
\textbf{Evidence.} \code{getRecordMetadata} (\code{NodeCommandsService} line~64) requires an authenticated caller but performs no party check, because the handover path reads the old share's token first. It returns the state, the lease token, both SAE IDs, and the pad length for a hash pair.

\textbf{Impact.} A pinned peer learns which pairs exist on a node and with which SAE. That is reconnaissance for \hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}. No pad material is returned.

\textbf{Scores.} \code{CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:H/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N} $=$ \textbf{2.7}. \code{CVSS:4.0/AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:H/UI:N/VC:L/VI:N/VA:N/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N} $=$ \textbf{5.1}. STRIDE: I.

\textbf{Accepted:} the handover path reads the old share's token through this call, so a party check would break the handover. Only pinned SAEs can call it (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}).

\section{Operational countermeasures}\label{sec:countermeasures}
The findings that are not accepted by design are covered by the following countermeasures. Each one names the finding, who applies it, and the mechanism.

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.2em]
\item[{M-1 Authenticated ciphertext transport (users; \hyperref[f:F-02]{F-02})}]\phantomsection\label{m:M-1} Carry ciphertexts only over an external, secure, authenticated channel between the two users, and decrypt only messages whose sender that channel has authenticated. The same countermeasure covers the plaintext malleability: the authenticated channel rejects a modified ciphertext before it reaches \code{DECRYPT}.
\item[{M-2 KME link (SAE operator; \hyperref[f:F-03]{F-03})}]\phantomsection\label{m:M-2} Run the SAE--KME link on a private cable or segment that a network attacker cannot record. Once you KME starts support PQC algorithms, update source code to reject classical ones
\item[{M-3 TROPIC01 bus custody (manufacturer, operator, and user; \hyperref[f:F-04]{F-04})}]\phantomsection\label{m:M-3} Run \code{PAIRING} (\hyperref[m:M-6]{M-6}) on the operator's bench, on a unit that has not left the operator's custody since flashing, so that no recording of the SH0 session exists. Then close the devkit in a tamper-evident enclosure, or apply numbered tamper-evident seals over the TROPIC01 and the MCU--TROPIC01 traces, and record the seal numbers with the device. No board change is involved. The user keeps the device in their possession, checks the seals before entering the PIN, and treats a broken seal as a lost device: the device is retired and a new one is issued, because a new \code{PAIRING} on a tapped unit would run inside a session the attacker may already read.
\item[{M-4 Network exposure (SAE operator; \hyperref[f:F-06]{F-06})}]\phantomsection\label{m:M-4} Set a firewall allow-list for the registry \code{port} and \code{40000 + port} from the SAE directory with the peer addresses only, and a per-source rate limit on new TLS connections.
\item[{M-5 Readout protection (manufacturer; \hyperref[f:F-11]{F-11})}]\phantomsection\label{m:M-5} Set RDP level~2 with per-device \code{OEM1KEY} and \code{OEM2KEY} as the last production step (Section~\ref{sec:params}). Level~2 removes debug and readout and freezes the option bytes, so reading page~22 needs an invasive attack. Keep the OEM keys offline, never in the repository.
\item[{M-6 Mandatory pairing (manufacturer or operator; \hyperref[f:F-12]{F-12})}]\phantomsection\label{m:M-6} Run \code{PAIRING} before a device is issued (Section~\ref{sec:params}), so that the published SH0 key no longer opens an L3 session.
\item[{M-7 Production PKI (CA operator; \hyperref[f:F-13]{F-13})}]\phantomsection\label{m:M-7} \texttt{CertGenerator} is lab-only. For production, the CA issues certificates as described in Section~\ref{sec:mit-ca}: \texttt{KeyUsage(digitalSignature)} and EKUs (\texttt{serverAuth} plus \texttt{clientAuth} for nodes, \texttt{clientAuth} for devices), \texttt{pathLen=0} on the CAs, separate PKCS\#12 passwords, offline CA keys, and random serials.
\end{description}

\subsection{CA and truststore architecture}\label{sec:mit-ca}
The SAE Node is the only component that needs both CAs: the client CA on the USB side and the SAE CA on the RMI side. The SE stores no client CA. It stores only the SAE CA (for \code{PROVISION}) and the owner pin (for USER sessions). The architecture:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Two TLS contexts in the Node, never merged.}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item The USB device-server context trusts \code{client_ca} only.
  \item The RMI context trusts the SAE CA only.
  \end{itemize}
  The Node builds them separately (\code{createContextForCommandServer} and \code{createContextForNode}).
\item \textbf{RMI peer pinning} on top of the CA check (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}), so that even a mis-issued SAE-CA leaf is not accepted as a peer.
\item \textbf{Optional further split.} \code{root-ca} is both the RMI anchor and the CA the SE trusts in \code{PROVISION}. A deployment can issue separate ``SAE-RMI'' and ``SAE-provisioning'' CAs to limit the blast radius of one CA key; the SE accepts any CA the owner installs.
\item \textbf{Role-binding extensions} (EKU, KeyUsage, path length) in the issued certificates (\hyperref[m:M-7]{M-7}).
\item \textbf{Offline CA keys} with distinct passwords, kept off the SAE hosts. A production UserApp never holds \code{client_ca.p12}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Production CA requirements.} \code{CertGenerator} is lab-only (\hyperref[f:F-13]{\fid{F-13}}). A production PKI meets at least these requirements:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Separate CA keys.} Use one key and one root (or an intermediate under an offline root) per trust domain: SAE-RMI, SAE-provisioning (the CA that \code{CREDS SAE} installs on devices), and device/owner client certificates. Generate each key in an HSM or on an offline host, with distinct credentials and custodians. Never place two CA keys on one host under one password.
\item \textbf{CA certificates.} \code{BasicConstraints CA:true} (critical) with \code{pathLen=0} for issuing CAs, \code{KeyUsage keyCertSign, cRLSign} (critical), Subject and Authority Key Identifiers, random serials of at least 64 bits, and a validity matched to the key-rotation plan.
\item \textbf{Leaf certificates.} \code{BasicConstraints CA:false}, \code{KeyUsage digitalSignature} (critical), and \code{ExtendedKeyUsage} by role:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item SAE nodes: \code{serverAuth} and \code{clientAuth} under the SAE-RMI CA, and \code{serverAuth} only under the provisioning CA;
  \item devices and owners: \code{clientAuth} only.
  \end{itemize}
  The subject or SAN carries the SAE ID or device ID, so that it can be compared with the SAE directory.
\item \textbf{Relying parties.} The Node trust managers check the issuer and the SPKI pin, and the SE installs the \code{CREDS SAE} certificate as given. Neither checks EKU or \code{CA:true}; the role extensions document intent, and the separation is enforced by the issuer, the pins, and the owner's choice of CA (\S\ref{sec:ca}).
\item \textbf{Lifecycle.} Have a documented issuance and revocation procedure. Publish a CRL for \code{client_ca} leaves. Rotating an SAE key means updating the pins on every peer. For a lost owner key, run \code{OWNER REPLACE} with a new key, because revocation at the CA does not reach the SE (\hyperref[f:F-08]{\fid{F-08}}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Residual risk}\label{sec:residual}
\subsection{Accepted by design}
\begin{itemize}
\item The PIN is the last line of defence: 8--16 characters and \textbf{7} online tries, after which M\&D destroys the KEK. At the minimum length of 8 digits, the chance of guessing it is $7/10^{8}$. Under model~B, an attacker with L3 access, a recorded PIN entry, and the owner key can reset the tries (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}).
\item The owner key alone can retarget \code{PROVISION} (\hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}, a product decision), but cannot decrypt.
\item The \code{OWNER SET} retry after a failed enrollment (\hyperref[f:F-05]{\fid{F-05}}), closed by the enrollment check.
\item The re-provision handover: a pinned non-party can delete a share and read any pair's metadata (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}, \hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}).
\item Owner identity by SPKI pin only, without chain, expiry, or revocation checks on the SE (\hyperref[f:F-08]{\fid{F-08}}).
\item \code{PAIRING LOAD} with the owner key and \code{pairing-key.hex} on an empty NV pairing record (\hyperref[f:F-09]{\fid{F-09}}).
\item Read-only USB console commands are unauthenticated (\hyperref[f:F-10]{\fid{F-10}}).
\item The owner-pin, \code{se_nv_has_owner()}, and time-floor decisions are single branches without duplicated checks (\S\ref{sec:sca}).
\item The GTZC and MPCBB configuration is not locked, and MPCBB1 relies on its reset default (Table~\ref{tab:map}).
\item Invasive readout of SRAM remanence on a chip that is kept from booting, which bypasses the erase on reset and tamper (\hyperref[c:C-8]{C-8}, \S\ref{sec:sca}).
\item One \code{root-ca} anchors both RMI and the SE's \code{PROVISION} check (\S\ref{sec:ca}).
\item The OEM regression keys. Their holder can regress RDP and replace the NonSecure image with the secrets kept (\hyperref[ta:TA-9]{TA-9}).
\item The SAE sees pad plaintext in RAM before sealing it to the device.
\item A power cut during consume burns a pad.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Remaining with the operational countermeasures in place}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ciphertext integrity.} Ciphertexts carry no integrity tag. Their authenticity rests on the external channel (\hyperref[f:F-02]{\fid{F-02}}, \hyperref[m:M-1]{M-1}).
\item \textbf{The KME hop is CLASSICAL TLS.} Its confidentiality under model~B rests on the private link (\hyperref[f:F-03]{\fid{F-03}}, \hyperref[m:M-2]{M-2}).
\item \textbf{Plaintext secrets on page~22,} protected against non-invasive readout by RDP~2 (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}, \hyperref[m:M-5]{M-5}).
\item \textbf{No field update.} A future SE defect stays in every issued unit until the \code{OEM2KEY} holder regresses and reflashes it. Replacing the Secure image erases the device secrets and requires a new enrollment.
\item \textbf{Invasive and fault attacks on the STM32U535.} It has no hardware crypto and no glitch detectors, and its software ML-DSA and ML-KEM are exposed to power and EM analysis. wolfSSL has confirmed side-channel protection for ML-DSA in a later release; a firmware build with it reaches an issued unit only through the \code{OEM2KEY} regression and a reflash (\S\ref{sec:sca}). TROPIC01's countermeasures are vendor claims (\hyperref[ta:TA-1]{TA-1}).
\item \textbf{A compromised pinned peer SAE} stays inside the trust set for the records it is a party to (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}, \hyperref[c:C-2]{C-2}), plus the accepted handover capabilities (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}, \hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}).
\item \textbf{Pin configuration.} The SAE directory (\code{SAE_NODES_FILE}) is a trust anchor. Write access to it on a node is equivalent to choosing its peers. Every site needs an identical copy, distributed out of band; the node refuses a group- or other-writable file.
\item \textbf{Manual revocation} leaves a window between a loss and its revocation.
\item \textbf{Model~B on TROPIC01 L3.} The SPI bus runs over devkit traces that cannot be changed. Possession, the seal, and \code{PAIRING} in custody are its only controls (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}, \hyperref[m:M-3]{M-3}). An attacker who defeats them, or who takes \code{pairing-key.hex} from the home PC, gains L3 access to TROPIC01, but not pads, the PIN, or device secrets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Locked SE (RDP~2).} Pads require the owner key and the PIN, and are out of reach of the NonSecure world, an arbitrary USB host, or the SAE. The owner key anchors every management action, including the SAE CA. Its protection on the home PC therefore matters for pad \emph{integrity}, but not for the confidentiality of pads already on the device. The remaining SE findings are accepted by design (\hyperref[f:F-01]{\fid{F-01}}, \hyperref[f:F-05]{\fid{F-05}}, \hyperref[f:F-08]{\fid{F-08}}--\hyperref[f:F-10]{\fid{F-10}}) or covered by the production parameters (\hyperref[f:F-11]{\fid{F-11}}, \hyperref[f:F-12]{\fid{F-12}}). TROPIC01's X25519 L3 session is protected under model~B by custody and a seal (\hyperref[f:F-04]{\fid{F-04}}, \hyperref[m:M-3]{M-3}).
\item \textbf{Lab SE (RDP~0).} SWD reads all MCU state. It is used only for development.
\item \textbf{Production SAE.} Pure-PQC mTLS with disjoint USB and RMI truststores. At the application layer, the caller identity comes from the pinned TLS peer and not from arguments (\hyperref[c:C-1]{C-1}, \hyperref[c:C-2]{C-2}), the node binds one address (\hyperref[c:C-3]{C-3}), and call arguments, return values, and the registry stub are allow-list filtered (\hyperref[c:C-4]{C-4}). The remaining RMI findings are Low or Info: handshake reachability (\hyperref[f:F-06]{\fid{F-06}}), covered by the firewall allow-list, and the accepted handover deletion and metadata reads (\hyperref[f:F-07]{\fid{F-07}}, \hyperref[f:F-14]{\fid{F-14}}). On an untrusted world-wide network, the node runs with the SAE directory distribution, a firewall allow-list, and a production CA in place of the lab \code{CertGenerator} (\S\ref{sec:mit-ca}).
\item \textbf{Users.} Ciphertexts travel over an authenticated channel (\hyperref[m:M-1]{M-1}).
\item \textbf{Home PC.} It has no listener. The main risk is theft of the owner PKCS\#12 together with its password.
\end{itemize}
